# Case Study 8 — ATC Transcription & Research-Based Verification

> **Portfolio note:** This is a reconstruction of the type of ATC transcription and review work I have done. I am not using proprietary audio, project instructions or original submissions.

## This wasn't really just a transcription project

When I first started the ATC work, it sounded like a transcription job.

It really wasn't.

Getting the words right meant researching what I was hearing. ATC audio can be fast, compressed and hard to hear, and there are a lot of things that sound completely different if you don't know the aviation context around them.

If I couldn't clearly make out a call sign, waypoint, airport reference or aviation term, I couldn't just type what it sounded like and move on.

I might need to check FAA information, airport diagrams, STARs, waypoints, historic flight data or other aviation sources to figure out what actually made sense in the context of the transmission.

So the work became a combination of **audio transcription, research and verification**.

## What I was researching

Depending on the audio, I could end up looking at almost anything connected to the flight or airport.

That included things like:

- airline and aircraft call signs;
- flight numbers;
- airport identifiers;
- runways and taxiways;
- waypoints and fixes;
- STARs and other published procedures;
- airport maps and diagrams;
- FAA databases and reference information;
- historic flight information for the date/time of the recording;
- routing;
- and aviation terminology I wasn't familiar with.

The research wasn't separate from the transcription. A lot of the time it was how I confirmed the transcription.

---

## An unclear word can turn into a research problem

Say I hear a controller give an aircraft an instruction and one part sounds like a waypoint, but I can't make the word out clearly enough to be certain.

I may have two or three possible things I think I heard.

I wouldn't want to pick the one that sounds closest and call it done.

I could look at:

1. What airport or airspace is this happening in?
2. What route is the aircraft flying?
3. What STAR or procedure would make sense for that flight?
4. Are any of the possible words actual waypoints in that area?
5. Does the rest of the transmission support one of them?
6. Does historic flight information help confirm the route or aircraft involved?

Sometimes that research makes the answer pretty obvious.

If the procedure shows a waypoint with the name I thought I heard and it fits exactly where the aircraft is in the sequence, I now have much better support for the transcription than I did from the audio alone.

But I still don't use research to invent something that isn't there.

The research helps me figure out what I am hearing. It doesn't give me permission to replace the audio with what I think the pilot or controller *should* have said.

---

## Call signs were another big one

Call signs can be especially tricky because the audio may be clipped, numbers can run together and the same aircraft may be addressed multiple times across the recording.

If I am unsure, I can use the surrounding information to help verify it.

For example, I might compare:

- the call sign I think I heard;
- the airline/operator naming convention;
- the flight number;
- historic flight information;
- the departure and arrival airports;
- and other transmissions involving the same aircraft.

If the first transmission is hard to hear but the same controller calls the aircraft again more clearly later, that matters too.

I used the whole recording, not just one isolated line.

## Airport diagrams and procedures mattered

Sometimes the easiest way to tell whether a transcription makes sense is to look at the airport itself.

If I hear what sounds like a runway or taxiway instruction, I can check the airport diagram.

Does that runway exist? Does that taxiway exist? Does the combination make sense based on the layout?

The same thing applies to arrival procedures. If the controller or pilot references something that sounds like a STAR or waypoint, I can look at the published procedure and see whether the names and sequence line up with what I am hearing.

That doesn't mean every unusual transmission is wrong. It means I have another piece of evidence to use instead of guessing.

---

## Historic flight data could help connect the pieces

The recording date and time could also matter.

Historic flight information could help me verify things such as whether a flight number was operating, what route it was flying or which airports were involved.

That was useful when the audio by itself gave me part of the answer but not enough to be comfortable with the transcription.

Again, I didn't want to turn "probably" into "definitely" just because I found a flight that looked right.

I still needed the audio and the research to make sense together.

## What accuracy meant to me

A transcription can be 99% readable and still have the important part wrong.

If I get a normal word slightly wrong, that may be one type of error. If I get the call sign, runway, waypoint or number wrong, I may have changed the meaning of the transmission.

So I paid a lot of attention to the small aviation-specific pieces.

I would ask myself things like:

- Is that really what I heard, or am I filling in what I expected to hear?
- Can I verify the term?
- Does it make sense for this airport or route?
- Did the same aircraft get addressed somewhere else in the recording?
- Am I using research to confirm the audio or to override it?

That last one is important.

## Moving into review work

As I got more experienced with the project, I also moved into reviewing other ATC submissions.

At that point I wasn't only checking whether another person's transcription looked clean.

I was checking the same kinds of things I had learned to check in my own work: Did the call sign make sense? Is that actually a waypoint? Does the airport diagram support the runway/taxiway reference? Does the historic flight information line up? Did the transcriber research an unclear aviation term, or did they just type something that sounded close?

I also had to be careful not to "correct" somebody just because I would have worded something differently. If their transcription was supported by the audio and the available evidence, that mattered more than whether it matched my first impression.

That progression into review work made me even more aware of how important it was to have a reason for a correction.

## Research didn't remove ambiguity

Sometimes, after all of that, the audio was still unclear.

That is part of the work too.

Research can narrow the possibilities and help confirm a difficult term, but it can't always turn bad audio into certainty.

If I couldn't support something, I didn't want to pretend I could just because I had spent time researching it.

That is very similar to how I approach other AI evaluation work now: **plausible isn't the same as verified.**

## What this work taught me

The biggest thing is probably that I stopped thinking of transcription as simply listening and typing.

For ATC, I was constantly moving between the audio and outside information:

**Listen → identify what is unclear → research → go back to the audio → verify → listen again**

And sometimes repeat that several times for one short section.

That combination of listening, research and checking my own assumptions is what made the work interesting to me.

The goal wasn't to produce the transcription that sounded most likely.

**The goal was to produce the transcription I could actually support.**
